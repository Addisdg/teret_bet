# Pilot Family Read-Aloud Review Guide

## Purpose

This review checks whether the five illustrated pilot stories sound natural,
hold a young child's attention, feel emotionally safe, have enough room for
every story beat, and use pictures that support both languages. It is not a test
of the child or the adult reader.

Do not record names, contact details, exact birth dates, photos, audio, video,
medical information, or anything else that could identify a child. Use only an
anonymous session code and the broad age band `3-4` or `5-6`.

Reviewer roles, anonymous coding, recruitment order, evidence handling, and
escalation are defined in `REVIEWER_OPERATIONS.md`.

## Pilot stories

1. The Rat Who Prepared Early — ages 3-4 and 5-6.
2. The Cub and the Kid — primarily ages 5-6; trust/safety focus.
3. The Rat King's Son — primarily ages 5-6.
4. The Lion and the Mouse — ages 3-4 and 5-6.
5. Stone Soup — ages 3-4 and 5-6.

Use the versioned illustrated PDFs linked from
`PILOT_REVIEW_PACKET_R1_2026-07-05.md`. Read the Amharic version first. Test the
English version in a separate sitting when possible so switching languages does
not distort attention or pacing.

## Recommended evidence

Before text lock, each story should have:

- at least three independent adult read-aloud reviews;
- at least one session in every applicable age band;
- at least one reviewer other than the owner;
- written notes for every hesitation, spontaneous rewrite, confusion, fear, or
  request to continue;
- explicit owner approval after all material notes are resolved.

This is the recommended pilot minimum, not a request to recruit the full closed-
test group yet.

## Before the session

1. Choose one story; do not test all five in one sitting.
2. Use a calm, familiar setting and normal reading voice.
3. Tell the child only that you will read a story together.
4. Do not explain the moral, unfamiliar words, or expected reactions in advance.
5. Keep the scorecard out of the child's attention.

## During the read-aloud

The adult should mark the page number whenever:

- wording feels unnatural or is changed aloud without planning;
- a sentence needs a second reading;
- the child asks what a word, action, or character choice means;
- attention drops or the child wants to skip ahead;
- a transition feels missing or a page feels overcrowded;
- a moment creates delight, prediction, repetition, or a request to continue;
- conflict appears frightening, shaming, or unsafe;
- the illustration beat would not explain or support the page.
- the picture conflicts with either language version or omits the main action;
- a recurring character, object, home, landscape, or clothing detail changes;
- cultural or setting details feel inaccurate, generic, stereotyped, or out of
  place;
- an expression or composition makes the scene more frightening than the text;
- the important action would become unclear in portrait or landscape cropping.

Do not quiz the child. Natural comments and reactions are more useful than
correct answers.

## After the story

Ask no more than three gentle questions:

1. “What part do you remember?”
2. “How did the story make you feel?”
3. “Was there any part you wanted to see or hear differently?”

For younger or quieter children, pointing, choosing an emotion face, or asking
to hear a page again is valid feedback.

The adult should also note spontaneous visual reactions: pointing, naming a
character, laughing, asking what an object is, mistaking one character for
another, or using the picture to predict the next event. Do not ask the child to
rate artistic quality.

## Story-specific watch points

### The Rat Who Prepared Early

- Is `አይጠ መጎጥ` understood as a small rat?
- Is the pretend shivering funny rather than confusing?
- Does the practical preparation ending still feel like one continuous story?

### The Cub and the Kid

- Does the predator/prey context create fear?
- Is playing from opposite riverbanks understandable?
- Does respecting distance feel concrete, or does the ending become too abstract?
- If adults must explain the trust lesson repeatedly, replace the candidate
  rather than forcing it through revision.

### The Rat King's Son

- Is the Creator -> Cloud -> Wind -> Mountain -> Bush Rat sequence easy to
  follow and enjoyable to repeat?
- Is the family/marriage frame comfortable for this age?
- Does any transition need another page?

### The Lion and the Mouse

- Is the falling/tangled-rope problem clear without being frightening?
- Does the rescue have enough time to feel earned?
- Does the child understand why the lion changes his view of the mouse?

### Stone Soup

- Does the traveller feel playful and safe rather than deceptive?
- Is the move from closed doors to shared work believable?
- Should any contribution receive its own page?
- Do the food and visual-setting notes feel culturally coherent?

## Decision rule

A story may advance to `Read-aloud approved` only when:

- no unresolved safety or cultural concern remains;
- adult readers no longer rewrite phrases spontaneously;
- children can follow the main cause-and-effect sequence without coaching;
- page breaks feel natural and no beat is cramped;
- Amharic and English each work as complete tellings;
- pictures support both tellings without unresolved mismatch, continuity,
  cultural, safety, or crop concerns;
- the owner explicitly approves the recorded evidence.

Use `Revise` for fixable wording, pacing, or page-flow issues. Use `Replace` if
the core premise remains frightening, confusing, culturally uncertain, or too
far from the sourced story after age-appropriate softening.
